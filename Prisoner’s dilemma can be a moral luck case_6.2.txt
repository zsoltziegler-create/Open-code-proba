Prisoner’s dilemma can be a moral luck case
When Betrayal Functions as a Form of Luck
ABSTRACT:
I seek to expand the conceptual framework of moral luck by introducing a specific category of
partner-luck, a situation in which two autonomous agents, for example, the participants in a Prisoner’
s
Dilemma (PD), are mutually dependent while lacking any genuine control over one another’
s
decisions. Although moral luck has traditionally been associated with the uncontrollability of natural,
accidental, or historical factors, a structurally analogous form of luck may also arise when the
outcome hinges on another agent’s free yet unpredictable choice. This form of partner-luck
illuminates how PD-type interactions can acquire a distinct moral dimension, wherein the divergent
outcomes of “cooperation” and “defection” yield not only rational consequences but also morally
charged evaluations.
To illustrate my point, I present a rescue scenario in which cooperative behavior (C) results in moral
success only if it is reciprocated by the other party; otherwise, the agent attempting to cooperate may
be subjected to severe professional and moral criticism. I then juxtapose the moral architecture
revealed by this example with the foundational tenets of the Control Principle and classical theories
of moral luck. While proponents of the Control Principle maintain that Adele (the cooperating agent)
cannot be held morally responsible for Bruno’s (the defector’s) independent decision, I contend that
the structure of moral appraisal in such cases is isomorphic to canonical forms of moral luck, I argue
that the very concepts of interpersonal injustice and betrayal, from the victim’s perspective, can be
reinterpreted as species of a broader genus: partner-luck.
My thesis is that the notion of moral luck should not be confined to blind accidents but can be
extended to dilemmas of social cooperation, particularly within game-theoretic structures like the
Prisoner’s Dilemma. In such contexts, the unpredictable autonomy of others may function as a
morally significant form of luck, shaping both outcomes and the moral standing of the agents
involved.

Introduction: Moral Luck and the Control Principle
1Page 4 of 16
The concept of moral luck arises when I morally evaluate an agent, whether to praise or to blame,
despite the fact that the action or its outcome is, at least in part, determined by factors beyond the
agent’s control (Nagel, 1979; Williams, 1976; cf. Nelkin, 2025). While many maintain that it would
be morally unjust to hold someone accountable for circumstances over which they exert no influence,
both everyday moral practice and the criminal justice system frequently appear to take outcomes,
often contingent upon chance or external conditions, into account. This phenomenon is what the
literature refers to as moral luck. The core question in the ensuing debate is whether, and to what
extent, it is appropriate, or even necessary, for moral appraisal to consider those aspects of action or
consequence that elude the agent’s effective control (Nelkin, 2025).
At the heart of the controversy lies the so-called Control Principle (CP), which stipulates that
individuals can be held morally responsible only for what lies within the scope of their control (Kant,
1784/1998; see also Nagel, 1979). The principle is often expressed in the form of a corollary: “ Two
people ought not to be morally assessed differently if the only other differences between them are due
to factors beyond their control” (Nelkin, 2025). The tension becomes apparent here: strict adherence
to the Control Principle seems to clash with ordinary moral intuitions, which often factor in actual
outcomes when evaluating agents (Williams, 1993). This is not merely a theoretical tension; it bears
significant practical implications as well, ranging from the moral and legal judgment of criminal acts
to mundane distinctions between the “unlucky” and the “fortunate.” It raises the troubling question
of whether we ought to discern a moral difference between two agents who are equivalent in intention
and capacity, yet whose actions lead to markedly different outcomes (Zimmerman, 2002).
The philosophical literature on the subject spans a broad spectrum. Some argue that moral luck
reflects a deeply embedded feature of our moral practices, thus implying that the Control Principle
holds only in a limited sense. Others contend that most instances of moral luck are best explained as
misperceptions arising from epistemic limitations or asymmetries in information and therefore
believe that the Control Principle retains much broader applicability (Richards, 1986; Wolf, 2001).
Whether moral luck constitutes an irreducible paradox, an expandable category of moral theory, or
merely a misjudgment in need of correction remains an open question (Nelkin, 2025).
At the outset, it is crucial to clarify my argument’s scope and aim. This paper does not seek to convert
the staunchest defenders of the Control Principle, for whom the very category of moral luck is
incoherent. Instead, it addresses those who already take the problem of moral luck seriously,
accepting the legitimacy of its canonical forms (e.g., resultant or circumstantial luck). My central
question is an internal one: if one accepts these traditional categories, what principled reason could

there be for rejecting a structurally analogous form of luck that arises from the contingent decisions
of our partners in cooperative enterprises? I do not aim to extend deontic blame for outcomes beyond
an agent’s control. Rather, I offer a diagnosis of the social ecology of moral appraisal, a domain in
which outcome-sensitivity persists as a stubborn feature of our practices, even when formal
responsibility can be rightly attributed elsewhere. The problem of partner-luck, as I frame it, is not
about who is ultimately to blame, but about whose moral standing is at stake.
The problem of moral luck, then, lies in the fact that an agent’s moral appraisal can be substantially
influenced by external variables over which they exercise no control, yet they nevertheless receive
differential praise or blame due to these uncontrollable circumstances. The classical illustrations of
this involve “blind luck”, as when a pedestrian suddenly crosses a driver’s path, or factors rooted in
genetic, circumstantial, or historical determinants. However, more recent discussions have begun to
consider the possibility that similar dynamics may arise in the context of human cooperation. Here,
the misjudgment or error of another agent, whose decisions fall outside the subject’s control, might
exert such an overwhelming influence that it engenders a situation tantamount to moral luck. While
traditional paradigms of moral luck tend to emphasize random externalities, one might plausibly
argue that another person’s morally defective decision may function analogously to an accidental or
environmental factor.
1. When the Prisoner’s Dilemma Becomes a Case of Moral Luck
The aim of the following thought experiment is twofold: first, to illustrate the essential structure of
the classical Prisoner’s Dilemma (PD) through a realistic emergency scenario; and second, to
elucidate how the problem of moral luck may arise when agents are unable to exert control over one
another’s actions. In the scenario, two paramedics working in an intensive care unit, Adele and Bruno,
are dispatched to the site of a mass-casualty accident. Their individual strategic decisions, whether to
cooperate or to defect, will influence not only the number of lives ultimately saved, but also the
subsequent moral evaluation of their conduct. The possible outcomes in the scenario (i.e., the number
of patients each manages to rescue) are isomorphic with the standard logic of the PD: mutual
cooperation (C, C) yields better results for both than mutual defection (D, D), yet from the standpoint
of individual advantage, defection may emerge as a dominant strategy.

Let us assume that the two paramedics, Adele (A) and Bruno (B), are professionally equivalent in
rank, training, and experience. There exists no superior-subordinate hierarchy between them, nor are
there any initial external conditions that would favor one over the other. Upon arriving at the scene,
they encounter a single medical container fully equipped for trauma care. For the sake of operational
efficiency, they may choose either to divide the equipment and work separately or to coordinate their
actions.
Both agents are incentivized to maximize the number of patients they individually stabilize and
evacuate, as these figures will later influence assessments by hospital administrators, media
narratives, and public opinion. Each faces the same strategic options:
Cooperate (C): Both commit time and effort to the coordination of tasks, operate under a
shared protocol, divide the equipment equitably, and synchronize their interventions.
Defect (D): Both abandon the joint plan, opting instead to pursue independent, competing
methods of rescue without genuine collaboration.
The situation is structurally symmetric: either agent may choose to cooperate or defect. The numerical
outcomes correspond to the number of patients each can stabilize and evacuate. The resulting payoff
matrix, consistent with the PD framework, unfolds as follows:
1.1 Cooperative Strategy (C, C): Collective Optimization and Its Limits
If both Adele and Bruno choose to cooperate, that is, to work together according to a coordinated
plan, the rescue process becomes more time-consuming due to coordination costs (e.g., negotiating
equipment use, delays in communication). Nevertheless, the synergistic effects of teamwork enable
them to handle more complex cases, resulting in three successful rescues per person, six in total. This
outcome constitutes the optimal collective result. Both agents receive professional recognition, as the
transparency and discipline of their joint action maximize systemic efficiency. However, as the PD
logic dictates, this strategy remains unstable: if one agent anticipates that the other will cooperate, it
may become rational to unilaterally deviate to secure a higher individual payoff.

1.2 Defective Strategy (D, D): Individual Rationality, Collective Loss
If both agents defect, opting for uncoordinated, independent action, the duplication of effort, logistical
interference (e.g., both responding to the same cluster of victims), and absence of communication
severely diminish operational efficacy. In this case, each rescues only one patient, for a total of two.
While defection may appear rational from the standpoint of individual decision-making (since 1 > 0
if the other defects), the collective outcome is dramatically inferior to that of mutual cooperation (2
< 6). This scenario epitomizes the classical paradox of the Prisoner’s Dilemma: the aggregation of
individually rational decisions results in a collectively suboptimal outcome, one that is worse for both
parties than if they had cooperated.
1.3 CD/DC: One Cooperates, the Other Defects
Let us imagine an emergency scenario in which two paramedics, Adele and Bruno, begin their
operation under identical conditions, equipped with equal resources and professional competencies.
Both are aware that mutual cooperation would reliably result in each saving three individuals.
Accordingly, Adele adheres strictly to the jointly agreed-upon plan: she organizes the equipment,
coordinates the intervention protocols, and acts cooperatively, trusting in Bruno’s assistance. Bruno,
however, perceiving Adele’s methodical and time-consuming organizational efforts, abruptly decides
to abandon the cooperative strategy. He deviates from the plan, seizes the equipment Adele had
prepared, and swiftly proceeds to rescue the most easily accessible casualties. In the end, Bruno
succeeds in saving five individuals, while Adele, hindered by a lack of tools and logistical disarray,
rescues no one.
At first glance, Bruno’s conduct is morally objectionable: he breaks an agreement, opportunistically
exploits his partner’s good faith, and undermines the foundation of future cooperation. Adele’
s
choice, conversely, reflects commendable integrity. Yet, the brutal reality of outcome-focused
evaluation, prevalent in professional contexts, media narratives, and public opinion, creates a
paradoxical situation. From an outcome-focused perspective, which privileges the sheer number of
lives saved, Bruno’s action could be framed as pragmatically superior, while Adele’s well-intentioned
but failed effort might attract moral criticism for its ineffectiveness. Her moral tragedy lies precisely
in being ensnared by a system of appraisal where her partner’s moral failing manifests as her own
professional failure. This example powerfully illustrates how adherence to cooperation can be
retrospectively judged as a form of ethical negligence, not because it is inherently wrong, but because
its success is vulnerable to the defection of others.

It is important to stress that the example does not aim to depict Adele as morally superior, nor Bruno
as malevolent or selfish. Quite the opposite: the cooperative-defective binary functions merely as a
model for examining how moral luck can arise between two individuals of comparable competence
whose initial cooperative intentions may nevertheless be derailed by strategic interdependence. It is
entirely conceivable that agents with identical motivations, expertise, and dedication may nonetheless
receive different moral evaluations solely because one of them happens to face a partner whose
decision, non-cooperative yet ultimately effective, falls outside the other's sphere of control. The
critical issue, then, is not who the “better” or "worse” person is but how outcomes that are random or
only partially controllable can come to dominate retrospective moral judgment.
So-called moral luck pair cases (Levy, 2016, p. 127) have frequently served as key instruments in
analysing the contours of this problem. In one case, luck significantly alters the agent’s moral
development or the outcome of her action; in another, luck is irrelevant or morally inert. What unifies
these traditional cases is that, aside from the luck variable, all other morally salient features are held
constant.
Let us now consider the situation in which Adele’s original intention was to engage in mutual
cooperation, which she reasonably assumed would yield a stable and predictable rescue outcome for
both parties. In this context, the relevant locus of luck, or rather, the factor beyond Adele’s control,
is Bruno’s decision to defect, a choice that Adele could not influence or anticipate. Following the
logic of standard moral luck pair cases, I may now imagine two possible outcomes:
Bruno cooperates: In this case, Adele’s efforts prove successful, and both paramedics rescue
three individuals. Thus, despite acting in exactly the same way (by cooperating), Adele
receives significant praise and recognition owing to the favourable result.
Bruno defects: In the actual scenario, however, Bruno exploits the joint plan for his own
benefit, appropriating resources while Adele invests her energy in futile attempts at
coordination. Ultimately, she saves no one and becomes morally suspect: “ Why was she not
capable of acting more independently and effectively?”
In both cases, Adele’s intention, effort, and ability remain identical. The only morally relevant
difference lies in the seemingly contingent behaviour of Bruno. Yet this single divergence exerts a
drastic influence on both the outcome and Adele’s moral standing, thereby exemplifying the essence
of moral luck, namely, that an agent’s moral status can be shaped decisively by externalities or by the
autonomous decisions of others, which lie beyond the agent’s control.

In line with defenders of moral luck, one may argue here that it is not Adele’s intention, but the actual
number of lives saved that determines the moral appraisal of her conduct. The force of the argument
mirrors that found in classic resultant luck examples, such as the pair case involving two hired
assassins. Suppose both possess identical intentions and prepare equally for their acts, but one fails
to kill because a bird randomly flies into the bullet’s trajectory, while the other successfully executes
the murder. Advocates of moral luck insist that the moral condemnation applicable to the latter cannot
be identically applied to the former, as the death of an actual person constitutes a morally weightier
fact than an unrealized intention.
The same intuition can be extended to the case of Adele and Bruno. A moral luck theorist would
maintain that one cannot reasonably offer identical moral praise to Adele1 (the one whose cooperation
is matched by Bruno’s) and Adele2 (whose partner defects), given that in the former scenario she
succeeds in saving three lives, whereas in the latter she saves none. The mere presence or absence of
cooperation from another agent, a variable beyond Adele’s control, suffices to radically alter the
moral evaluation of otherwise indistinguishable actions.
2. The Theoretical Core: A Full Defense of Partner-Luck
The strongest objection to the very idea of partner-luck is that it rests on a basic category mistake: it
treats a moral agent’s deliberate choice (Bruno’s) as on a par with a blind, morally neutral natural
event. While this criticism looks compelling, it misfires on several levels because it ignores the
epistemic character of my argument and the internal logic of the moral-luck debate. My case for
partner-luck is a multi-tiered defense that builds directly on the manuscript’s framework of “partner-
luck” within PD-type interactions.
2.1. First Level: The Phenomenology of Radical Contingency
7Page 10 of 16
My starting point is not a naïve ontological identity claim, but a precise description of Adele’s
phenomenological and epistemic position. From Adele’s perspective, the eventual upshot of Bruno’s
decision is radically contingent. Although Bruno is a moral agent with reasons, those reasons sit for
Adele inside an opaque “black box,” the other mind. At the moment of choice, despite shared history
and rational trust, the partner’s sovereign freedom introduces into the situation a variable that is
unknowable and beyond Adele’s control. In this respect, the complex neurochemical processes in
Bruno’s brain that eventuate in his decision constitute, for Adele, a no-less-unpredictable segment of
the world’s causal fabric than a sudden gust of wind or a quantum fluctuation.
Let us be precise about the nature of the analogy, as the strongest objections are likely to arise here.
The argument is not a crude ontological reduction that equates the moral content of a rational decision
with the moral neutrality of a natural event. Rather, it is a claim about epistemic opacity and causal
impact. (For further decision theory analyses, see Chapter 3, Formal Analogies in Decision Theory.)
The crucial point is that from Adele's deliberative position, her partner's impenetrable "other mind"
functions as a “black box.” The internal logic of his reasons, values, and intentions is inaccessible
and ultimately uncontrollable. The thesis thus bypasses the metaphysics of Bruno’s freedom to focus
on the phenomenology of Adele’s vulnerability. This establishes why it is justified to describe the
situation in terms of luck: for Adele, her partner's decision impinges upon her course of action with
the force of an external contingency.
However, the purpose of this epistemically focused analysis is not to absolve Adele from all
consequences of the outcome. On the contrary: its goal is to precisely describe the mechanism through
which moral luck operates. The fact that Bruno's decision is an epistemic black box for Adele still
carries a new, irrevocable moral-ontological consequence for the result of her actions. It creates the
fact that ”she saved zero lives,” and for the proponent of moral luck, moral appraisal is precisely
sensitive to such actual outcomes. Adele’s moral standing becomes a hostage to a causal process that
is, for her, functionally indistinguishable from chance, yet whose result nevertheless irrevocably
adheres to her moral record.
Whatever Bruno’s choice metaphysical source (libertarian freedom or complex determinism), the
decision’s unpredictability renders it functionally equivalent to an external natural contingency. What
we face is not natural chance, but a form of agential contingency. This maps onto the manuscript’s
claim that another player’s free yet unpredictable choice can serve as a locus of luck in PD-like
settings.
2.2. Second Level: The Primacy of Epistemic Framing
8Page 11 of 16
A critic might reply that this is merely Adele’s subjective standpoint, whereas the objective moral
fact is that failure was caused by a responsible agent. This objection misses the point: the thesis of
partner-luck is explicitly epistemic. I do not assert that Bruno is ontologically a rock slide. I contend
that, from Adele’s limited and intractable epistemic position, Bruno’s decision impinges as an
external, unpredictable, and uncontrollable event, and moral appraisal (both that of observers and
Adele’s self-assessment) tracks that outcome and the uncontrollable factor that produced it. This is
precisely where the problem of moral luck takes root: in situations where an agent’s epistemic horizon
comes apart from her causal reach. The manuscript’s framework emphasizes that, in such PD-type
interdependence, another agent’s volition can operate as the relevant “beyond-control” variable that
moral evaluation nevertheless responds to.
2.3. Third Level: Safeguarding the Internal Logic of the Debate
A more sophisticated objection charges that by centering outcomes and Adele’s perspective, I am
smuggling consequentialism into a dispute where intentions should matter. But this misconstrues the
debate’s dialectical structure. The problem of moral luck is by definition the pressure point where a
fundamentally deontological commitment, the Control Principle, collides with our entrenched, often
outcome-sensitive moral intuitions. The debate would simply not exist without the intuition that
actual outcomes bear moral weight. To fault a partner-luck argument for exhibiting outcome-
sensitivity is therefore to attack the very precondition of the problem’s existence. My aim is not to
convert devotees of the Control Principle, who reject the category of moral luck from the outset, but
to show those who take the moral-luck problem seriously that partner-luck is a hitherto neglected yet
paradigmatic case of it. If one accepts that a randomly flying bird can generate resultant luck in a
classic pair case, then logical consistency requires accepting that a partner’s epistemically opaque,
sovereign decision can do the same. The question here is not the legitimacy of consequentialism, but
the range of possible sources of luck within a framework that already treats outcomes as morally
relevant. This aligns with the manuscript’s expansion of moral luck from natural accidents to inter-
agent dependence in PD-type structures
2.4 The Core Defense: From Responsibility to a Revisionist Moral Ontology
My defense of partner-luck must confront a series of increasingly sophisticated objections. By
addressing them sequentially, I can reveal the full philosophical implications of my thesis, which
ultimately proposes a revisionist account of certain core moral concepts.
According to the first objection, the “Responsibility Exhaustion” thesis claims that my case fails the
basic test for moral luck. The concept of luck, it is argued, is needed only to explain a responsibility
remainder (Williams, 1976), a morally significant outcome not fully accounted for by the actions of
9Page 12 of 16
responsible agents. In Adele’s case, there is no such remainder. The situation is perfectly explained
by Bruno’s betrayal and Adele’s status as a victim. Responsibility is cleanly allocated, the moral
description is complete, and the category of luck is explanatorily redundant.
This objection, however, rests on a CP-centric view of the problem. For those who take moral luck
seriously, the “responsibility remainder” is not causal but evaluative. It is the stark difference in the
moral standing of Adele₁ (who saves three lives) and Adele₂ (who saves none), despite their identical
intentions and actions. The clean allocation of blame to Bruno does not erase the negative outcome
that adheres to Adele₂’s record. It is this evaluative deficit that the concept of luck is required to
explain. Note that, for any proponents of the control principle, there is no residual responsibility in
any moral luck example. The analogy with circumstantial luck makes this clear. The Nazi
environment was created by responsible agents, yet we still consider it a source of moral luck for the
young German raised within it. If the possibility of blaming the source were enough to neutralize
luck, then circumstantial luck itself would be an incoherent category.
The second objection says the “Sufficient Moral Description” Thesis. A more refined critique,
operating from within the moral luck framework, concedes the first point but draws a new distinction.
It argues that luck is a concept reserved for morally mute causal inputs, like a bird flying into a bullet's
path. Bruno’s betrayal, by contrast, is a morally rich event, for which our vocabulary, injustice,
betrayal, wrongdoing, is perfectly sufficient. Since there is no descriptive deficit, there is no need to
invoke the category of luck.
This powerful objection leads to the radical core of my thesis. I argue that concepts like “injustice”
and “betrayal” do not compete with “partner-luck”; rather, from the victim's standpoint, they are
species of it. The philosophical contribution of my analysis is to reveal the hidden, luck-based nature
of these interpersonal moral phenomena. By becoming the victim of an injustice, an agent's moral
narrative is irrevocably altered by a factor, another's sovereign will, over which they have no control.
This reveals a profound truth: one's fundamental commitment to either the CP or to moral luck
determines how one understands the very meaning of concepts like injustice. For the CP-adherent,
injustice is a self-contained dyadic relation of perpetrator and victim. For the moral luck theorist, it
is also a manifestation of the victim’s profound vulnerability to agential contingency.
The third objection is the “Paradox of Moral Stance.” This leads to the final and deepest challenge.
If injustice is a form of luck, the critic argues, my entire normative framework collapses. To see
Bruno's act as an injustice requires us to adopt a participant stance (in Strawson’s sense), holding him
responsible with reactive attitudes like resentment. To see it as a source of Adele's luck requires us
10Page 13 of 16
to adopt an objective stance, viewing his decision as an external, unpredictable event in the world's
causal fabric. One cannot simultaneously hold both stances toward the same agent regarding the same
act. The thesis, therefore, demands a performatively contradictory and psychologically impossible
moral judgment.

3. Formal Analogues in Decision Theory
Within the framework of causal decision theory, CDT (Bacon, 2022), Bruno’s decision represents,
for Adele, an external factor no less uncontrollable than, for instance, a pigeon flying into the path of
a hired assassin’s bullet: the outcome hinges on it, yet the agent has no power over it. Likewise, in
evidential decision theory, EDT (Ahmed, 2014; Gallow, 2024), although agents might form
expectations about others ’behavior based on evidential correlations or statistical probabilities, this
does not amount to genuine control over the other’s conduct; it remains a loose co-variation, not a
mechanism of influence. Thus, both CDT and EDT affirm that while agents lack direct control over
one another, the other’s behavior may nonetheless bear immense moral weight in determining
outcomes. That is the very structure of moral luck.

resolved but is shown to be an ordinary, structural, and perhaps inevitable feature of our moral
experience in any interdependent social setting. It reveals that moral judgment, far from being a
sovereign act of rational appraisal, may itself be the residue of a strategic dilemma.
